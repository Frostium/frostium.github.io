*October 20th, 2023*

**Contributions to Modern Psychology**  
Psychology is analogous to a flower: out of the roots of theologians and ancient philosophers spread the shoots of enlightenment philosophy which finally grow into the bright petals of modern science. Several philosophers stand out: Blaise Pascal, John Locke, and Immanuel Kant. Blaise Pascal is most famously known for three things: mathematics, theology, and philosophy, but his contributions to psychology are manifold. (Jerphagnon, n.d.) John Locke, the 17th century philosopher, is well-known for his impact on the founding fathers of America, but also contributed to psychological theory. (America’s Survival Guide, n.d.) Immanuel Kant wrote philosophy in Germany during the 17th and 18th centuries and is known for synthesizing empiricism and rationalism among other additions to modern psychological science. (Teymoori, 2017\) Together, these three men formed the basis for much of modern psychology.  

Pascal believed people were best convinced by their own logic. “People are generally better persuaded by the reasons which they have themselves discovered than by those which have come into the mind of others.” (Pensees) How then can anyone be convinced by another person? Pascal argues that a persuader must be subtle, leading their audience into the conclusions rather than dragging them. Centuries later, psychology recognizes empathy as a key element of good persuasion. (Popova, 2016\)  

Christianity has long held that our loves should be ordered according to importance: God first, then our neighbors, and so on. Pascal applies this concept to psychology, arguing that a balanced and happy person will order their attention toward truly valuable subjects. He writes, “The sensibility of man to trifles, and his insensibility to great things, indicates a strange inversion.” (Pascal, 1965, Section 198\) Today, many psychologists encourage structuring life around meaning and order, a notable example being Jordan Peterson. (Peterson, 2002\) 

“Pascal’s wager” remains the most controversial argument posited by the philosopher. In *Pensees*, Pascal argues that a rational decision maker will choose to believe in God even if the odds are against him. He bases his position on his work in probability, noting that “if you win, you win all; if you lose, you lose nothing.” (Pascal, 1965, section 233\) Psychology now has an entire field of study dedicated to decision making. (Cherry, 2022\)  

Descartes believed humans had “A priori” truths. (Baehr, n.d.) Locke contested his position, formulating a concept called the “tabula rasa” or “blank slate”. He believed that humans were born with no personality or knowledge and gained all through experiences. “ Experience… In that all our knowledge is founded; and from that it ultimately derives itself.” (Locke, 1962, Book 2 Chapter 1 Section 2\) This belief is known as “empiricism”. Locke’s emphasis on experience, although not common now, laid the groundwork for psychology’s understanding of human beings. (Martin, n.d.)  

Locke proposed that the self is a “conscious thinking thing, … which is sensible, or conscious of pleasure and pain, capable of happiness or misery, and so is concerned for itself, as far as that consciousness extends.” (Locke, 1962, Book 2 Chapter 27, Section 19\) Our identity then, according to Locke, transcends our bodies and minds. Even if it denies the eternal aspect, Psychology still recognizes the unique aspects of the  mind’s ability to be “aware” or “conscious” and understands that our identity is founded in what we are aware of about ourselves.   

In *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Locke comments on how memories change human personality. “As far as consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person.” (Locke, 1962, Book 2 Chapter 27 section 11\) Locke’s influence continues today, as psychologists emphasize the importance of memories to a person’s identity. (Psychology Today, n.d.) As an example, certain diseases like dementia cause memories to be forgotten and the patient to take on a new character.   

Kant believed that the soul was unable to be introspectively observed. (Leary, 1982, p. 21\) He is often credited with the “copernican revolution” of thought: as Copernicus took the earth’s movement into account with respect to the universe, so Immanuel Kant took the mind’s movement into account with respect to knowledge. (Teymoori, 2017\) He believed the mind took an active part in constructing knowledge and meaning from the senses. His views synthesized rationalism and empiricism by stating that both have a part in knowledge acquisition. (Leary, 1982, p. 24\) Psychology still benefits from his teachings through considering mood, physical circumstances, sense impairment, and many more factors when analyzing why a person knows what they know. 

As corollary to this concept, Kant pioneered an early form of functionalism. The many systems in a human (sense perception, memory, reason, etc.) all work together to create a whole being. Thus Kant believed that the “I” was not reason or experience alone but a synthesization of everything in that person. Although functionalism is no longer the prevailing theory, most psychologists today hold a similar stance about the multi-faceted identity of humans. (Brook, 2020\)  

One of Kant’s great contributions is called the “Categorical imperative.” It reads very similar to Jesus’s teaching (Holy Bible, Matt. 7:12) colloquially referred to as the “Golden Rule”. Kant posits that people should “act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.” (Kant, 1983, p. 11\) In essence, a person should not do to anyone else what they wouldn’t want done to them. This fundamental rule still impacts Psychology through the *Law of Reciprocity* which claims a biological and social basis for the golden rule. (Kalman, 2011\)  

Pascal, Locke, and Kant added no small contribution to modern psychology. From decision theory to memories to the golden rule, these three men formed the basis for the modern study of the mind. Each in turn carefully reasoned and sought to find wisdom. None of them achieved perfection, but all generously watered the plant of knowledge. Their gardening has cultivated the flower of modern psychology.
